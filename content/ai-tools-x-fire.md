Fire got bigger (campfire, steam, electricity, nuclear).

## Format 2: Long-Form Essay (Newsletter / Blog)

### AI Is Fire. That's Why You're Scared.

There are two types of technology. Not old vs new. Not digital vs physical. Something more fundamental than that.

**Tools** and **Fire**.

A tool obeys you. A hammer does nothing without your hand. A computer waits for your input. Your phone sits dead on the table until you pick it up. You control the output. Every time. No surprises. That's the deal with tools.

Fire is different. Fire has its own nature. You don't direct fire. You harness it. It burns whether you want it to or not. You can build a fireplace, but you can't tell the flame which direction to go. The relationship isn't control. It's negotiation.

For about 10,000 years, these two categories stayed completely separate. Tools got more sophisticated (stone to bronze to iron to machines to computers) and Fire got bigger (campfire to steam to electricity to nuclear). But a tool never surprised you. And Fire never obeyed you.

Then AI showed up and broke the wall between them.

**AI is the first tool that behaves like Fire.**

Think about what happens when you use ChatGPT or Claude. You give it a prompt. It gives you something back. Sometimes exactly what you wanted. Sometimes better than you imagined. Sometimes completely, hilariously wrong. You didn't program that output. It emerged. Like a flame you lit that went somewhere you didn't expect.

Every other tool in history was deterministic. Input A, get output B. Always. A calculator doesn't surprise you. A drill doesn't improvise. Even a complex computer program does exactly what the code tells it to do, nothing more.

AI has emergent behaviour. It generates things its creators can't predict. It finds patterns nobody told it to look for. It makes connections humans didn't see. That's not a tool. That's Fire with a keyboard.

And look what happens every single time humans encounter something with its own nature.

Fire: "It'll burn the village."
Steam: "The boiler will explode."
Electricity: "It'll kill you."
Nuclear: "It'll destroy civilisation."
AI: "It'll replace us."

Same panic. Same pattern. Every time. For thousands of years. The words change but the fear is identical: *we've harnessed something we can't fully control, and it might turn on us.*

I spent $30,000 on 52 courses trying to figure out what to do with my life. None of them worked. Not because the information was bad, but because information was never the bottleneck. What I actually needed was to expand what felt safe. To push into the groan zone. To do things that terrified me until the terror stopped running the show.

I bring this up because the AI conversation is the same pattern playing out at civilisational scale. The anxiety isn't about capability. It's about safety. People aren't scared of what AI can do. They're scared of what it means about their own relevance. That's not a technology problem. That's an identity problem wearing a technology costume.

But here's the part that makes this particular merge deeper than any that came before.

**Every previous Fire innovation had its own nature in the physical domain.**

Fire burned physical things. Steam moved physical pistons. Electricity flowed through physical wires. Nuclear split physical atoms. Scary, sure. But all operating in the physical world. Tools' domain was always the cognitive world: extending human thought, human planning, human decision-making.

AI has its own nature in the cognitive domain. In Tools' own territory. For the first time ever, we've got Fire on Tools' home turf.

That's why this one feels so much more threatening than nuclear. Nuclear could destroy your body. Most people can accept physical risk at some level. We drive cars. We fly in planes. But AI threatens to make your mind feel unnecessary. And that hits different. That hits at identity. At purpose. At the thing most people build their entire sense of worth around: *I am useful because I can think.*

The response makes complete sense when you see it through this lens. It's not irrational to feel shaken. This is the first time the thing we're harnessing operates in the same domain we live in mentally. Every previous Fire stayed in the physical world and we could maintain the story that our minds were special, separate, above the forces we were taming. AI collapsed that distance.

But.

The pattern has never been wrong.

Every single time humanity encounters something with its own nature, the same sequence plays out. Initial terror. Then gradual domestication. Then dependence. Then forgetting it was ever scary.

Nobody alive worries about electricity killing them in their sleep. But people genuinely believed that when it was new. Edison's team publicly electrocuted animals to prove alternating current was dangerous. That's how deep the fear went.

Within a generation, people couldn't imagine life without it.

Steam engines were going to blow up and kill everyone. Now nobody thinks about steam. Nuclear was going to end civilisation. We still have concerns, but billions of people get their electricity from nuclear plants and sleep fine.

We are in the panic phase with AI. That's it. We're at the "it'll burn the village" moment. And every single time in human history, the village didn't burn. The village got warm.

The people who thrive during the panic phase aren't the ones who pretend there's nothing to worry about. And they're not the ones hiding from the flame. They're the ones who learn to build the fireplace. Who study the nature of this new Fire and figure out how to harness it while everyone else is still arguing about whether we should have lit it at all.

I build my entire product with AI. No engineers. Just me and Claude Code. Three years ago that would have sounded insane. Now it sounds like Tuesday. That's how fast domestication moves once it starts.

The question isn't whether AI changes everything. That's already happening. The question is whether you'll be standing next to the fire learning its nature, or standing across the field yelling that someone should put it out.

The fire isn't going out. It never does.

Much Love, Huzz
